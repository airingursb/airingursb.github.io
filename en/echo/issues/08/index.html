No. 08 / Self & everyday life

Let the plan branch. Give the next step somewhere to land.

You have imagined many paths, but each feels like starting over. How can today connect with what comes next?

Let long-term direction accommodate change. Keep it connected through short actions and reviews.

A plan should help me keep judging, as well as help me follow through.

Bochuan's long letter looks back on repeated changes of direction. I center this exchange, alongside earlier advice on promotion and later reflection on anxiety about milestones. The essay is my present organization of the material; blue quotations preserve the translated historical replies.

01 Reading notes

See how decisions connect before adding another task list.

Bochuan wrote at length about study, projects and job hunting. Repeatedly, he invested in one direction and turned elsewhere after encountering difficulty. At the end, he identified two problems himself: major decisions lacked investigation, and later development lacked groundwork. The first thing I saw was this discontinuity, not merely a day on which tasks went unfinished.

A denser schedule might only organize the next round of busyness more neatly. The deeper question is why each step is worth taking and where it leads after completion. The number of courses watched differs from the ability to deliver a real project. Collecting certificates may not bring someone closer to the role they want.

That is why my reply addressed planning, reflection, perspective and ability. Planning cannot work alone. Investigation before a decision supplies its basis; observation during action reveals what does not fit; review brings new information into the next choice. Without these, a plan can be a wish with a more formal name.

Begin with one recent important decision and complete three statements: why do it, what will it leave behind, and where will that be used next? If a connection is missing, investigate that link. Compared with filling the whole future at once, this makes it easier to see whether present effort lays groundwork or merely offers temporary reassurance that you are not idle.

When this step is done, where will it lead?

02 Reading notes

Write the distant plan as several conditional paths.

I suggested an annual theme and an updated five-year plan at the annual review, with three to five possible branches over the longer horizon. Those numbers described an approach offered to one reader, not an exact standard for everyone. The approach addressed a tension: needing direction without being able to know every change in advance.

Branches do not mean scheduling every desirable outcome simultaneously. I would add conditions to each: which information would make it worth pursuing, what resources would need preparing, and when should it be reviewed? Otherwise, keeping possibilities open can become several full-time plans competing for the same time, with none progressing.

For example, one long-term interest might lead to a project at work or first to a smaller creation outside it. The opportunities required differ, yet some learning and preparation may be shared. Recognizing the common part makes it possible, before the final choice is clear, to do something useful to more than one direction.

The point is to leave room for the unknown. What cannot yet be established should be written as a condition to verify, rather than quietly promoted from wish to promise. A longer horizon needs more permission to change. It helps me see possible connections; it does not require the future to arrive in the order on today's page.

Several possible roads need not be several simultaneous sprints.

03 Reading notes

Identify the departure first. Then change the action or the plan.

In the reply I specifically asked for reflection when a plan goes off course: was it unexpected, was something missed while planning, or does the plan itself need updating? These questions matter because the same outward failure to follow a route may call for very different responses. “Poor execution” cannot usefully combine them all.

If an expected opportunity did not appear, another branch may be needed. If a task is more complex than expected, its steps and timing may need adjustment. If contact with reality shows that the goal no longer fits, the goal itself may change. Continuing and adjusting should both have specific reasons.

I suggested a daily or weekly journal recording what happened, shortcomings, how to improve and tomorrow's plan. Short review cycles ground judgments in facts. Rather than reach year-end with only an overall sense of defeat, notice early that an arrangement repeatedly stalls, while there is still time to address the cause.

The extension I offer here is to preserve two kinds of information at each review: changes that have already occurred, and the adjustment to try next. On returning, you can compare whether the adjustment helped. The plan then becomes more than a document for rewriting goals; it can accumulate experience and gradually improve judgment.

Different causes call for different adjustments.

04 Reading notes

Let milestones support preparation. Do not let them define a person.

In my earlier reply to Yuanshan, I stressed learning the promotion requirements and preparing before the opportunity arrived. Later, writing to Yanxia, I admitted having scheduled life's milestones too tightly and becoming anxious whenever I seemed unlikely to reach one. Read together, the letters show tension in my understanding of planning rather than a position that was always seamless.

Preparation with an object and a time can help action. But if one milestone comes to carry the whole meaning of whether I am still accomplished, any surprise can threaten the self. I would examine that milestone again: does it still serve the life I want, or am I now simply maintaining a feeling of not falling behind?

So keep branches in the long term, define actions in the short term, and make room between them for review. The next action can be concrete: ask someone to verify a requirement, complete a small project, or divide a repeatedly postponed task. Concrete does not mean unchangeable. Knowing what an action is meant to test makes revising it less likely to erase direction.

I do not expect a plan to remove every uncertainty. It is useful if it helps me notice changed conditions earlier, recognize what I have accumulated when turning, and understand why I continue when I choose to. The distant route can change while today's step still lands. Neither needs to invalidate the other.

Use milestones to prepare, not to pass judgment on a life.

A little time for the present

Does rest need a reason?

Finally, some free time. Then the worry that the day was of no use.

When we are used to planning for the future, how do we give some time back to the present?

I do not have a finished answer about time either.

In the previous issue, I wrote about thinking one move ahead when choosing a job. Here I want to consider the other side of that habit: if we are always investing in the future, must even an ordinary afternoon prove useful first? This essay revisits three exchanges from 2025. Quotations translate my replies from then; the surrounding essay is my reflection now. Issue numbers do not describe the order in which these thoughts occurred.

01 Reading notes

Work has ended. The mind is still racing.

Yanxia wrote that he could leave work on time and had more free hours, but often felt drained when he got home. A game, a few videos, and the day was over. He did have something he wanted to do: writing a novel was an old wish, and he had begun again. What hurt was loving it while suspecting it was not worthwhile. Once the time was spent, he seemed to owe himself a result that could demonstrate progress.

He was frank about the unease beneath this: he feared others would think he was not good enough. Sometimes he wanted to work a little longer at night, as though that would protect his standing. Calling the problem a lack of discipline after work would miss this. We can leave our desks while continuing to sit an examination in our heads. Adding another plan may not help us relax.

I did not reply from the position of someone who had solved it. While building my own product, I deliberately found a quiet, comfortable place to live, then worked from waking until late at night and barely went out. The surroundings changed; the voice urging me onward stayed. A choice intended to improve life ended up improving the conditions for more work.

Reading these letters now, I would separate two questions: is there something I want to experience in my free time, and can I let myself experience it without an achievement to show? Yanxia already knew he loved fiction. The difficulty went beyond finding an interest to allowing that interest a period of inexperience and no return.

Fatigue, boredom and fear of time passing may need different responses. Rest when tired; take time with what you want to make. The fear of falling behind in other people’s eyes deserves attention of its own, so that it does not silently decide every evening for us.

02 Reading notes

What do we lose by pricing every hour?

I recognize Yanxia’s worry: if something proves meaningless, will it waste the lead we have already built? I once planned my career around precise milestones. As a deadline approached without a clear way to meet it, I grew anxious. I began assessing everything by whether it helped the next step. That approach did help me pursue opportunities. It also made unplanned time harder to accept.

Even reaching a goal did not bring lasting calm. In my reply, I described feeling empty after an achievement, then planning another goal. One completed milestone often began another round of pressure. If today’s peace depends on a future result, today is always waiting for the next report card.

We can therefore weigh the costs of a job, as in the previous issue, and still want space in life. A job occupies a substantial part of our time; its pay, tasks and learning deserve scrutiny. A slowly read book or an unfinished novel has made no promise to carry our entire development. Asking it for the same proof of return extends that measure too far.

Besides, returns cannot always be seen in advance. In the letter, I acknowledged the limits of our present view: experiences that seemed pointless sometimes helped later, while some choices might indeed turn out badly. Accepting this does not require repackaging everything we enjoy as a long-term investment. That would merely postpone the due date.

I would rather ask whether the experience itself contains something I want to keep. Yanxia wanted to write a good novel; I wanted to make a good product. There was a particular kind of anticipation in both. We can spend some time on that without guaranteeing it will eventually earn money, win a prize or prove the choice was right.

Assessing a job need not mean assessing every minute of a life.

03 Reading notes

Where attention goes, what remains of life?

In another exchange, Weiguang made the same question smaller and more precise. Reading a book in traditional Chinese with vertical text, he had to return to sentences, and recover their meaning whenever his attention wandered. He kept feeling inefficient. Why, he asked, do we increasingly need to give things a reasonable purpose before we feel entitled to do them?

When that letter arrived, I was struggling too. Work filled my time so completely that his words made me suddenly realize the year was nearly over. Only after taking a day off did I have the energy to read carefully. Work messages still arrived on my day off. That day, I did not keep reading them.

I simply went out for food and a haircut, and discovered that shops I liked had gone and the familiar barber’s staff had changed. The neighborhood had kept changing without my noticing. In my reply I described the contrast: I was becoming more sensitive to work metrics and less sensitive to daily life.

I began to see a cost of efficiency that rarely enters the accounts: attention spending so long responding to only one kind of thing. Each day contains many completed tasks, yet afterward I struggle to say what I experienced. Life did happen. The attention that could receive it had been used elsewhere.

Weiguang’s slow reading, and the wind and sunlight he could feel again through a song, showed me another texture of time. These might add nothing to the day’s output, yet give it a recognizable feeling. I did not conclude that commitment to work must destroy daily life. I needed to acknowledge that, when work consumes all my energy, doing it faster will not automatically return those experiences to me.

Some changes become visible only when we stop.

04 Reading notes

Leave an afternoon open. Ask no report of it.

It is easy to give rest a respectable reason: working better tomorrow. Sometimes that is true. But if it is the only permissible reason, rest still serves the next output. A walk must work; reading must yield something; even daydreaming should ideally produce an idea. Arranged this way, the afternoon never quite belongs to us.

So I told Weiguang that looking at a sunset and letting my mind wander was, to me, a way of using time too. Those words “to me” matter. I can say how I want to experience time, without prescribing a superior way of living for everyone. Nor do I want sunshine and walks to become another checklist.

Income, responsibilities and the future still need consideration. Making room for the present does not erase them. It reminds me to include my present self when allocating time. Enjoying a story, eating a meal with attention, noticing the light change: these can be the whole of an experience, without having to lead to a further result.

In the earlier reply to Yanxia, I was clear that welcoming feelings and making room for experience was something I hoped to approach, far from something I had achieved. These exchanges contain no ending in which everyone stops being anxious. They leave moments I am willing to revisit: when anticipation became pressure, when accumulation crowded out experience, and when I might simply set down what I am doing for a while.

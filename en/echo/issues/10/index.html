No. 10 / Self and everyday life

I still cannot get started. Is my willpower to blame?

The task is always waiting, rest never feels restful, and self-blame makes starting heavier.

Separate the goal, the task and how you are feeling. Give both rest and starting a more definite place.

Before urging yourself to begin, notice what you are carrying.

In this summer exchange, Chuqing wrote about study, difficulty taking action and a difficult period personally. I have followed the question of getting started. Blue quotations translate the original reply; the practical questions in the essay extend that discussion.

01 Reading notes

Separate the things that have piled up together.

Chuqing wrote during the summer break. The study material was already there, but little progress had been made. Plans to finish assignments on the day they were set or visit the library the next morning kept giving way to other people, a phone or getting up late. The hardest part was seeing the same pattern repeat after each fresh resolution, and doubting oneself more each time.

The letter contained much more than a task. Regret about grades, plans for the next stage of study, difficulty starting and a low mood were all weighing on the same holiday. If I simply recommended a productivity tool, the list might look tidier, but those different kinds of difficulty would still be pressing on the same start button.

I first addressed what the reader wanted from university. Postgraduate study, studying abroad and preparing for work do not have identical priorities. With no direction settled, there is also room to discover interests and strengths. My original comment about the importance of grades was brief. To use that advice carefully, it is necessary to check what a particular goal actually requires, rather than generally treating grades as unimportant.

Since Chuqing had already expressed a wish for further study, the next questions could be: which courses and requirements need attention, and how does the material at hand connect to that goal? These questions extend the reply. The larger the ambition, the more useful it becomes to give one immediate task a specific place, so opening a book does not have to prove the worth of an entire life.

This does not require a perfect long-term plan. You could first name one thing you most want to move forward and a period this week you are willing to give it. The other questions have not vanished. They simply do not all have to demand an answer at the same moment. Deciding what you are addressing today gives the action a clearer boundary.

Which goal does today’s task serve?

02 Reading notes

Give the task a time. Give rest one too.

I admitted in the reply that I procrastinate too. Keeping a task in mind from the first day of a holiday, or opening a to-do app to look at it every day, is not the same as setting aside time for it. It is possible to do no work while feeling that you should not rest, ending up with neither a completed task nor a moment in which you truly set it down.

I used my own weekend as an example: rest on Saturday, then study on Sunday afternoon. That arrangement fit my life at the time; it was not a timetable for Chuqing to copy. The point was to stop pursuing unfinished tasks during rest, and to avoid deciding from scratch whether to work when the time to begin arrived. Each kind of time had a place.

I also mentioned deadlines. That was personal experience, not advice that leaving things to the last minute is better. Tasks involving collaboration, early feedback or repeated revisions cannot be judged only by whether they are submitted on the final day. In revisiting the reply, I would make the question more precise: when do you need a first version that you can continue working from?

For reading some material, that might mean choosing a time you can genuinely protect, reading to a sensible stopping point and noting the questions you have not understood. This develops the idea of arranging time; it is not a process the original letter claimed to have tested. Reducing one attempt to something whose progress you can observe gives the next arrangement a little real experience to draw on.

If the agreed time arrives and you still do not start, I would rather know what happened. Material that is too difficult, a noisy environment, an overloaded plan and energy that has not recovered call for different adjustments. Copying the list again may carry the same obstacle into the next day. Recording the obstacle gives you something specific to try changing.

Reserve a time to work, and a time to rest.

03 Reading notes

Look at the present state before passing a verdict.

Chuqing judged these habits harshly and asked how to get rid of them. I understand the desire for a cause when plans repeatedly fail to become action. But if the entire explanation becomes “I have poor willpower,” it neither identifies the hardest step nor does much to help arrange the next attempt.

So at the end of the reply, I returned to the reader’s state at the time. When someone already feels low, starting an undertaking that requires sustained effort with little short-term reward can take more energy. I did not want a fixed character label to stand in for that situation. Equally, I cannot determine every cause of the difficulty from one email.

I suggested a few days to recover some mood and energy before making a fresh arrangement. This was an experience-based response to that reader at that moment, not a promise that the difficulty would disappear after a certain amount of rest. Rearranging work also means accounting for real deadlines and responsibilities. What can wait and what needs attention can be considered separately; neither dropping everything nor carrying everything is the only option.

When starting again, I would rather examine a particular attempt than score the whole person. Was the time allowed enough? Was the first step still too big? After doing a little, was the next step clearer? If not, the arrangement can change again. That review at least leaves information behind, rather than merely another judgment of failure.

The reply offers no method for never procrastinating again. What I want to leave is a gentler and more specific starting point: acknowledge that things may be difficult now, distinguish what the task needs from what your present state needs, and choose an arrangement to try. One failure to begin need not become a final conclusion about you. The next attempt can still have different conditions.

Before you turn the page

Make room for one attempt.

What is the most useful condition to change before you next begin?

It could be the size of the task, a time slot, or some rest first. Choose one thing you can change now.

Record an obstacle. Hold back a verdict.

AI and mathematics

Breakthroughs, resistance and research roles

COVER STORY

AI released 700-plus manuscripts. Why are mathematicians divided?

On October 6, OpenAI released mathematical research generated by an internal model. Some saw an acceleration of discovery; others called for a boycott. What lies behind these different responses?

Our lead reading is Kazike’s essay on AI’s transformation of mathematics, alongside APPSO’s reporting and mathematicians’ original statements. Start with the event, then follow what changes in research.

What happened on October 6?

On October 6, OpenAI published openai/math, a GitHub collection of mathematical manuscripts and proof artifacts generated by an unreleased internal model. Existing mathematics evaluations had become less informative, so the company expanded evaluation to open research problems. The significant change is what the model was being asked to produce: arguments about unresolved questions, beyond answers to established exercises.

A manuscript is a research draft. It states a claim and its assumptions, then presents an argument. Public release allows scrutiny and follow-up work; it is not the same as completed peer review. The collection includes materials at different verification stages, and several documents can belong to one result: companion arguments, consequences or alternative proofs.

Prominent examples concern the Riemann zeta function, connected to the distribution of prime numbers; faster matrix multiplication; and a four-dimensional Kakeya problem. Readers need not master the details to see the significance. AI is attempting questions researchers have pursued, producing arguments that can be examined beyond how convincingly they read.

Corrections followed release. The initial 722 manuscripts became 719 after three withdrawals; the version used here groups them into 372 result families. Optimists see the scale of machine exploration. Cautious readers see a large body of material still requiring scrutiny. The dispute begins with both observations being possible at once.

Why this matters: AI is contributing paths into research

Earlier discussions of AI mathematics often centered on solving exercises with known answers or translating a human proof into a machine-checkable language. An open problem has no ready answer key. A system must find a viable argument, navigate obstacles and present something others can examine. Valid contributions to that process constitute research progress regardless of their machine origin.

Kazike’s most useful argument connects progress to the speed of feedback. Where results can be checked quickly, AI can attempt a route, encounter failure and try again. More directions become accessible within a given period. Mathematics and code attract attention partly because some outputs can be connected to automatic checks.

A missing step still matters: finding an argument, formalizing it and confirming that it addresses the intended problem are different jobs. The collection is the output of an evaluation and preparation process. The model contributed arguments; problem selection, earlier literature, checking tools and selection for release also shaped the result.

The prospect is larger than finishing one problem faster. Researchers could explore routes they previously lacked time to pursue, compare alternatives and extract useful methods. That is a substantive expansion of working capacity. A selected collection of successes, however, does not establish a general success rate for an arbitrary new research question.

What are the objections actually about?

The saved APPSO article brings another side into view: errors in the manuscripts, the release process and researchers’ concerns about their future roles. Its tension with Kazike’s essay is useful. One emphasizes consequences for the mathematical community; the other emphasizes the possibilities opened by AI capability. Neither is fully represented by its headline.

Two organizations matter. The Association for Human Mathematics, AHM, called on October 7 for mathematicians to discontinue work with OpenAI. It questioned whether this release model advances understanding. The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, AGMAI, is an independent group of scholars. It too objects to frontier research testing on inaccessible proprietary models, but continues discussions with labs and recommends better release practices and support for understanding.

“Mathematicians boycott AI” collapses these positions. Tao’s blog hosted an AHM-authored guest statement; that does not establish that he led its authorship. AGMAI’s advisory work is not an endorsement of the collection. The useful questions are who is speaking, what practice they object to and what they propose.

The withdrawals reported by APPSO are a real quality concern and should affect trust in unchecked material. They do not show that every result is false. Conversely, a valid proof does not settle every question about publication and collaboration. The common ground is that technical change is already affecting research work; the terms for evaluating and sharing that change remain contested.

Why doesn’t Lean settle the dispute?

Think of Lean as a language for stating mathematical definitions, claims and proofs precisely. Its proof kernel checks whether an argument follows the rules under its stated assumptions. This gives readers a way to check corresponding artifacts beyond trusting an author’s assertion.

But a successful check concerns the statement actually encoded. Consider a simple example: someone intends to prove that every integer has a positive square, but adds “the integer is nonzero” in the formal statement. The revised claim is true; the original fails at zero. The checker has not malfunctioned. The task changed. Research-level differences can be much subtler, so the original question, manuscript and formal statement must still be matched.

Correctness also leaves other questions open. An argument may rediscover a known result, or provide little explanation of its method. Kazike is right to draw attention to fast feedback as an accelerator. Treating the entire cost of research assessment as the cost of one check, however, leaves out literature review, explanation and application. Formalization coverage also needs an explicit scope.

These jobs need not remain exclusively human. A Lean kernel postmortem records AI-assisted bug finding, and models can also assist literature search, statement comparison and counterexample discovery. The promising technical direction improves generation and scrutiny together, producing reasons for trust that another researcher can reuse.

If proofs become cheaper, who supports understanding?

Imagine a doctoral student planning several months of work who discovers that AI has released a possible proof of the same problem. That may be good news for the field, while requiring a major change to the student’s project. They must check the result, then choose whether to change direction, extend it or learn its method. Receiving an answer and developing research ability happen on different timescales.

This illustrates why “more results must be better” does not end the argument. Explanations, discussion and use carry results into other people’s work. When output grows faster than the ability to assimilate it, attention shifts toward selection and understanding. Which results deserve attention first? Who explains them? How is that labor supported and recognized?

AGMAI proposes that labs support the assimilation of substantial output that is not yet well understood, with established nonprofit academic institutions directing funding decisions. Workshops, sustained working groups and exposition can help turn a released answer into usable knowledge.

Access raises a further question. A public proof lets others examine a result; accessible research tools let them pursue their own questions. Both matter in different ways. The worrying scenario concentrates agenda-setting and discovery inside a few labs, leaving others primarily to receive outputs. The preferable one expands the number of researchers able to explore directions of their own choosing.

Will this follow the earlier resistance to AI in art?

The comparison is understandable. A profession built around difficult skills encounters automation at scale; excitement coexists with concern about work and value. Artists and mathematicians can share that experience. But “there was resistance” is not enough to establish the analogy. We must ask what was contested and whether those conditions subsequently changed.

A closer mathematical precedent is Appel and Haken’s 1976 computer-assisted proof of the four-color theorem: four colors suffice for a planar map when regions sharing a boundary must differ. Extensive computation raised questions about both reliability and understanding. Thurston still used the example in 1994 to discuss mathematical understanding. A formal proof completed in 2005 strengthened the basis for checking. New mechanisms of trust developed without eliminating the desire for insight.

In the arts, the 2024 AI training statement objected to unlicensed use of creative works and its effect on livelihoods. Its claim was not simply that machines could never produce good art. Warner Music Group and Suno’s 2025 partnership announcement emphasized licensing and creator choice. It illustrates that adoption and negotiation can coexist. It does not establish that all artists benefited or that earlier objections became groundless.

Mathematical truth has checking methods unlike judgments of artistic value. Today’s AI also generates research arguments, reaching beyond earlier computer-assisted computation. The useful lesson is therefore a concrete institutional adaptation, rather than inevitable surrender: checkable proofs, accessible tools, explicit credit and sustainable training can turn concerns into better terms of use.

How can collaboration go beyond generating and reviewing proofs?

Imagining AI writing proofs and people reviewing them overlooks the cycle of research. Proofs suggest new questions; failed routes can leave useful methods. Models may help ask questions and explain arguments as well as solve them. A permanent boundary in which humans supply meaning and machines execute is therefore an uncertain foundation.

A more constructive relationship begins with an actual task. A researcher identifies something they want to understand; a model suggests possible routes. Examples, counterexamples and literature help eliminate unsuitable paths. Once an argument develops, its key ideas and checking materials are prepared for others to use. AI can participate throughout, while contributions remain open to questioning.

Students particularly need opportunities to develop judgment. I would allow model assistance with background and alternatives, while asking students to explain assumptions, identify a failed example and reason again when conditions change. That distinguishes obtaining a proof from learning to conduct research. Sanderson’s argument for recognizing explanatory work and Lozano-Robledo’s discussion of students both direct attention toward developing that capacity.

Academic incentives must support it. If recognition depends only on new-result counts, clear exposition, proof organization and teaching methods become under-rewarded support work. Better tools should increase the value of those activities too. Otherwise a larger volume of results can coexist with fewer opportunities for researchers to develop independently.

Section referencesGrant Sanderson · recognizing explanatory work ↗Álvaro Lozano-Robledo · addressing students ↗Leiden Declaration · community recommendations ↗

Beyond solving problems: building a shared practice of research

Kazike’s essay is the lead recommendation because it makes a consequential shift visible: AI is extending from assistance with reading and exercises into the production of research. That warrants both excitement and specific verification. APPSO and the original statements supply another part of the story: discussion, access and transferable methods help new results expand a field’s capabilities.

I would neither dismiss the dispute as wounded professional pride nor conclude that AI should stay out of research. Continued collaboration is more promising, with explanation, scrutiny and support built into release practices. Machines can contribute ideas; researchers need the means to examine, alter and build on them.

The next evidence to watch is independent scrutiny, follow-on research and access for students and researchers beyond the labs. Those developments will reveal whether the release mainly adds documents or expands usable knowledge. The remaining features pursue a related question: how capabilities become sustained work whose steps can be inspected and understood.

02

Agents taking on work

Memory choices and responsibility for actions

FEATURE

Why can an agent forget a request from two hours ago?

Two Pi engineering articles examine limited working context and the cost of repeatedly processing conversation history.

A long coding conversation can retain its messages and files while the agent loses track of an earlier constraint. Understanding why starts with the input to its next model request.

Compaction is a handoff that can lose information

Pi is a coding agent that reads files, changes code and calls tools. Its next model request includes instructions, tool definitions and a growing record of conversation and execution. The context window—the input a model can handle at once—is finite. A chat application can save more history than the model sees on any particular turn.

Pi summarizes older material while retaining recent messages, then continues with a shorter input. This is compaction. Think of a shift handoff: a brief carries goals, progress and key decisions, while original records remain available for details. The working input changes; the model’s parameters are not retrained.

Consider an editorial example. A user asks for Chinese and English versions of a weekly issue, initially as a preview. Hours later the summary says only “finish the issue page.” The Chinese page may be completed while the second language and preview scope disappear from working memory. Subsequent reasoning now starts from an incomplete task description.

Useful compression preserves what determines the next action: unfinished requirements, the basis for conclusions and unresolved questions. The shortest summary is not necessarily the best. Access to original records makes it possible to discover and correct a poor handoff.

Section referencesEarendil · Pi compaction ↗

Why can a short “continue” be expensive?

A second problem is repeated work. Each request can contain a long stretch of old input with only a few new sentences. Prompt caching reuses attention state computed for an identical beginning, reducing recomputation. This is reuse of computational work, not an additional store of trustworthy facts.

The identical beginning is crucial. If a request contains instructions, tool definitions and history, appending a message may preserve reusable computation. Changing a tool definition in the middle changes the prefix for all later history, even if those later words stay the same. The diagram marks that boundary.

Compaction therefore trades a shorter working input against a rewritten prefix. The first request after compaction needs to establish the changed continuation again. Frequent edits need not save money, while retaining everything consumes context. Decisions need to account for information quality, remaining work and the provider’s actual retention behavior.

Section referencesEarendil · prefix caching ↗

Count rework alongside input savings

An illustrative bill makes the distinction concrete. Start with 80k old tokens and 4k new tokens. If cached reads cost one tenth of ordinary input, a hit costs the equivalent of 12k ordinary-input tokens; a miss costs 84k. Both requests contain 84k actual tokens. The difference is billing and recomputation, not that the model suddenly sees seventy thousand fewer tokens.

Cache-hit rate is not the final outcome. If a summary drops the English edition, inexpensive subsequent requests still leave translation, review and user waiting to be paid for. A better summary may incur additional computation now while avoiding later rework.

I would assess long-running memory by whether it preserves the goal, revises judgments when evidence changes and retains access to their basis. Caching helps make that work affordable. The quality of the handoff determines whether computation still serves the right task.

FEATURE

Can an agent resume after crashing halfway through a task?

Pi Durable persists work for long-running agents. The difficult case arises when an external action has happened but its result has not yet been recorded.

An interrupted conversation can be restarted. A half-finished deployment, write or email cannot always be repeated. This week’s saved technical manual shows why.

Pi is a coding tool; Durable is an execution framework

On October 1, Earendil released the Pi 1.0 coding agent alongside an experimental package, Pi Durable. The former is a terminal tool used by a person; the latter is a framework for developers building long-running agent applications. It stores conversations, tool calls and unfinished tasks so another process can reopen existing work.

That matters because a model often chooses the next action from its current record. It needs established facts about completed actions and confirmed outcomes, rather than only “the process stopped; continue.” Persistence keeps those facts outside the model and available after a restart.

The manual demonstrates a forcibly terminated process with report retrieval marked safe and deployment marked unsafe. Reopening the store can replay retrieval, while deployment returns interruption information for subsequent handling. Here safe and unsafe describe replay semantics, not whether the tools are inherently good or bad.

Section referencesEarendil · Pi 1.0 and Durable release ↗Pi Durable · manual 1.0.3 ↗

An email exposes the difficult gap

Suppose an agent requests an email notification. The mail service accepts it, but the local process crashes before saving the receipt. After restart, the absence of a success record does not show that nothing was sent. Retrying can duplicate a message; skipping can lose a genuinely failed request.

The diagram places local records and external execution on one timeline. The difficult interval is after remote execution but before local confirmation. Applications need a queryable request identifier or a provider-supported idempotency key: an identifier that lets repeated submissions represent the same action. Model confidence cannot replace missing state.

This also explains the framework’s boundary. Durable records provide the basis for recovery; preventing duplicate effects requires cooperation from the specific external service. Saving a conversation does not automatically implement exactly-once execution everywhere.

Section referencesEarendil · Pi Durable ↗

Continuity matters because the work need not be handed over again

Together with compaction, persistence reveals two complementary records. A working summary carries the goal and remaining obligations; execution records carry completed actions and uncertain outcomes. Losing the first can derail the task. Losing the second can misrepresent the outside world.

For long-running work, I want confirmed work to continue, queryable outcomes to be checked and genuinely new choices or unresolved states to be surfaced. That reduces repeated handoffs while retaining control. Asking again at every step undermines delegation; concealing uncertainty lets problems accumulate.

Recovery should continue the original assignment. If the user requested a preview, the restarted agent completes that preview; publication remains a separate decision. Persistence makes a task more continuous. It does not silently expand its authority.

What is AI improving?

The limits of improvement and collaboration

FEATURE

Dream-RSI: using past experiments to plan the next attempt

The researchers turn code-optimization history into a replay environment, then use AI to revise the search policy. What does this “dreaming” save?

An agent can generate and test many versions of a program to make it faster. With a limited budget, choosing which route to try and which to pursue is a problem of its own.

The target of improvement is what to try next

Dream-RSI, a collaboration involving Google, Google DeepMind, the University of Maryland and others, studies how agents organize exploration. In code optimization, a modification creates a candidate program, a test returns a result and a later attempt branches from one candidate. Recording parent-child relationships produces a search tree containing promising results as well as unsuccessful routes.

AI revises the policy that decides which candidates to extend, how to allocate parallel attempts and when to stop a route. The underlying coding agent remains fixed. The analogy is the same experimenter working from a better experimental plan: changing where effort goes can improve outcomes without changing individual capability.

The research alternates real exploration, historical replay and policy revision, then returns the revised policy to live tasks. Experience helps decide how to collect the next experience. That feedback loop is the concrete meaning of recursive self-improvement here; it does not require retraining the underlying model each round.

This is useful because coding capability and the allocation of a task’s trial budget are different questions. Dream-RSI makes the latter an explicit object of improvement.

Section referencesDream-RSI · paper v2 ↗

Why is replay cheaper than rerunning experiments?

Suppose a recorded tree contains a modest route A, a promising route B and a failed route C. One policy repeatedly pursues A; another surveys all three before concentrating on B. Where visited nodes already have recorded outcomes, their choice sequences can be replayed to compare the use of a limited budget.

The saving comes from avoiding regeneration and retesting of already recorded candidates. Scheduling and policy evaluation still cost something, but the expensive underlying experiment need not be repeated for that node. The illustration uses the same tree to separate changes in search policy from changes in a program.

An unvisited node has no stored answer. History also reflects the old policy’s choices, including directions that received little attention. A replay advantage therefore needs testing on new tasks. Otherwise the system may become better at navigating an old map without becoming better at exploring unknown terrain.

Section referencesDream-RSI · project and original figures ↗

One experiment, two different gains

The project tests programs that solve Lasso, a regression method that penalizes coefficient size to select variables. The aim is to improve solver speed. It records discovery calls separately from average runtime on six held-out datasets, testing whether improvements carry into data not used for policy selection at that stage.

For Gemini-3.7-Flash, fixed exploration used 3,200 calls versus 1,879 for Dream-RSI, roughly 41% fewer. Average program runtime fell from 2,516.7 ms to 2,350.6 ms, about 6.6%. These measure different things: the investment in exploration and the performance of its output. They cannot be collapsed into one general speed multiplier.

The broader possibility is that AI can improve how it organizes work. Evaluation remains crucial. Runtime offers relatively clear feedback. For “write a good article,” a score based on length or output speed can reward exactly the wrong behavior. Whether the target captures what matters is the next question I would watch.

FEATURE

PANDA: how should a group of agents form a useful team?

This paper studies collaboration infrastructure: finding suitable partners, organizing communication around a task and continuing when a participant fails.

One agent searches, another analyzes data and a third checks code. Putting them in a shared conversation does not automatically create a team. They need discovery, handoffs and a way to replace missing participants.

Find collaborators, then form a small team

PANDA is a multi-agent architecture proposed by Northeastern University researchers. It considers a large population of independently managed agents with different tools and capabilities, assembled into temporary task teams. Routing discovery, scheduling and messages through one service can create a bottleneck and a common point of failure.

The design separates discovering available collaborators from communication within a selected team. Nodes maintain local capability indexes to find partners; a small team then establishes its task communication. A group discussing a specific problem need not involve the entire collective every time.

The source diagram shows responsibilities inside one node: a registry for peers, a team manager, a backend connecting the actual agent and failure detection. Decentralization redistributes management rather than eliminating it. That is an interesting direction for agents using capabilities across organizational boundaries.

Section referencesPANDA · paper ↗

Let the task determine the structure

The paper supports star, chain and mesh orchestration. A star uses a team-level coordinator and suits parallel collection; a chain suits ordered dependencies; a mesh supports repeated discussion and cross-checking at additional communication cost. The diagram’s letters are roles, not an intelligence ranking.

Consider this issue as an editorial example. One participant examines OpenAI’s release, another reads AHM and AGMAI, and a third checks historical analogies before synthesis. The value comes from obtaining different kinds of evidence. Three agents paraphrasing the same report can still inherit one shared omission.

Before adding an agent, ask what additional capability or material it contributes. For a short, self-contained task, another handoff may add only delay and misunderstanding. Where expertise and evidence differ, clear division of work can expand what one person can accomplish.

A team can keep running and still answer incorrectly

PANDA also studies recovery from crashes, network faults and failures in orchestration. Affected work can be replanned or reassigned. Its HotPotQA evaluation—a benchmark requiring information from multiple documents—reports completion and efficiency. The paper defines completion as producing an answer, regardless of whether that answer is correct.

That distinction matters. A missing member can be detected and replaced. Three members misreading the same source may look like a smoothly completed task. The evidence diagram separates whether messages arrive from whether a conclusion has independent support.

The paper’s trust network also constrains eligible collaborators; it is not a substitute for checking content. I hope this infrastructure makes complementary AI work easier to organize. Team quality should be visible in an added source, a discovered error or a task beyond the capacity of one agent, rather than in the number of assigned roles.
